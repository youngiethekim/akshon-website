Starcraft 2 was almost banned in Korea. When you think of Korean esports, Starcraft immediately comes to mind—the RTS that defined an entire generation and launched the esports boom. But few realize that Starcraft 2 nearly disappeared from competitive play entirely. This wasn't an accident; it was the result of a fierce corporate battle that almost destroyed competitive Starcraft in the region. The conflict between Blizzard, the organization KeSPA, and broadcaster GTV reveals how fragile esports infrastructure truly was.

## The KeSPA Monopoly on Korean Starcraft

For years, two television channels controlled Korean Starcraft: OGN (OnGame Net) and MBCGame. These broadcasters ran the OSL and MSL—the two most prestigious leagues in the world. Behind the scenes, KeSPA held absolute power. They controlled tournament operations, match scheduling, and broadcasting rights, creating an iron grip on the scene. This monopoly worked perfectly until someone dared to challenge them.

## How GTV's Challenge Triggered the Conflict

In 2008, a new channel called GTV launched the GomTV Classic with a revolutionary idea: English shoutcasting for Western audiences. Tasteless and SuperDanielMan brought Korean pro Starcraft to global viewers for the first time. The strategy worked, and GTV became wildly successful. Blizzard even sponsored a 50 million won tournament. But KeSPA didn't appreciate the competition. When GTV partnered with Blizzard instead of going through KeSPA, the organization pressured teams to withdraw. Seven teams pulled out in a single season, and GTV collapsed. This wasn't about overworking players—it was about maintaining control.

## Blizzard Took Direct Action Against KeSPA's Power

When Starcraft 2 launched in 2010, tensions exploded into the open. Blizzard wanted to work directly with teams and tournaments, but KeSPA refused to share power. In retaliation, KeSPA pressured South Korea's rating board to restrict Starcraft 2 to adults only, delaying the game's release. They also banned all KeSPA-affiliated players from competing in Starcraft 2 tournaments. Meanwhile, Blizzard partnered with GTV again and launched the GSL (Global StarCraft II League), bypassing KeSPA entirely.

## Starcraft 2 Survived Through Player Defections

The standoff couldn't last forever. As player contracts expired, top Brood War pros like Jaedong and Boxer defected to Starcraft 2. By 2012, KeSPA's power had weakened significantly. They finally agreed to work with Blizzard, and the Esports Federation—a new organization formed by non-KeSPA teams—ensured KeSPA could never reclaim their old monopoly. While GSL survived, the years of conflict had already damaged the scene's potential.
