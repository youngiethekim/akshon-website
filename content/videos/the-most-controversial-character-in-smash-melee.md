Ice Climbers Melee have earned their reputation as the most controversial character in competitive Super Smash Bros. Melee history. What makes them so unique and divisive? These twin climbers—Popo and Nana—operate as a single unit controlled by one player, with the secondary character mimicking inputs six frames behind. This dual-character design created possibilities no other fighter could match, fundamentally changing how the game was played.

## What Made Ice Climbers Melee So Powerful?

For years, Ice Climbers Melee remained mid-tier, ranked around 11th through 18th on competitive tier lists. But in 2004, players discovered desyncs—techniques that temporarily separated the two climbers and allowed players to control them independently. This opened entirely new gameplay possibilities, from guaranteed edge guards to elaborate combo setups. However, one technique would change everything: wobbling.

## Wobbling: The Infinite Grab That Broke Melee

Wobbling became the Ice Climbers Melee signature move that sparked outrage across the competitive community. When both climbers grabbed an opponent, players could perform rapid pummels and alternating tilts at a specific tempo (roughly 200 BPM), keeping the opponent locked in grab stun indefinitely. Once the victim reached a kill percent, a throw or finisher would end the stock instantly. No counterplay. No escape options. Just an automatic loss if the grab connected.

This technique catapulted Ice Climbers from obscurity to dominance. By 2006, they jumped from 15th to 7th on the tier list. Players like Chwat, Wobbles, and others proved the character's competitive viability, but the technique felt antithetical to Melee's core design philosophy—a game celebrated for having countless defensive options and comeback potential.

## The Ban and Evolution Beyond Wobbling

For years, wobbling remained legal in most regions but banned in others, creating inconsistency at majors. The debate intensified after Wobbles' legendary 2013 EVO run, where he defeated three "gods" and reached grand finals using the controversial technique. By 2019, the competitive community reached a breaking point. Broadcast viewership tanked when Ice Climbers players appeared, and top competitors expressed frustration with the degenerate matchups.

The Pacific Northwest implemented a compromise: eight pummels per grab maximum, forcing Ice Climbers players to evolve. Players like Slug and Lunard Dusk adapted by mastering handoffs—frame-perfect regrab techniques that required genuine skill and opened up defensive counterplay. Today's Ice Climbers meta is far more dynamic and respected, proving the character didn't need wobbling to remain competitive.
